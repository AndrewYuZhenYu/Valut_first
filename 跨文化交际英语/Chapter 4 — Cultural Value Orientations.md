# Chapter 4 — Cultural Value Orientations

Structured Knowledge Outline | Intercultural Communication

## I. Cultural Patterns: Basic Concepts

### 1. Definition of Cultural Patterns

A cultural pattern, also called a value orientation, refers to the values, beliefs, and other orientations that characterize the dominant group within a culture.

Different cultural patterns help explain differences between Chinese and Western cultures.

### 2. Components of Cultural Patterns

The presentation identifies four components:

- Thinking Model: The ways people think and organize their ideas.
    
- Values: Principles, standards, or qualities considered worthwhile or desirable.
    
- Norms: Standards established by cultural values.
    
- Social Practices: A component of cultural patterns identified in the courseware.
    

#### A. Thinking Model

Human thinking consists of eight interacting elements: knowledge, ideology, methodology, intelligence, emotion, willpower, language, and habits.

The presentation compares the following thinking modes:

|Western Thinking|Chinese Thinking|
|---|---|
|Rational|Intuitive|
|Analytical|Holistic|
|Objective|Subjective|
|Linear|Curvilinear|

Linear vs. Curvilinear Thinking

- Linear Thinking: The presentation characterizes native English speakers' thinking as linear.
    
- Curvilinear Thinking: Chinese discourse often develops in a spiral pattern. It may begin with background information, discuss the topic indirectly, and gradually approach the central point.
    

Chinese discourse is also described as emphasizing indirect and implicit expression.

#### B. Values

Definition: A value is a principle, standard, or quality considered worthwhile or desirable.

Four characteristics of values:

1. Values are the core of culture.
    
2. Values guide people within a culture.
    
3. Values apply to every aspect of life.
    
4. Values establish norms.
    

#### C. Hofstede's Onion Model

The presentation uses an onion to illustrate cultural layers.

Symbols

Words · Pictures · Gestures · Objects

Heroes

Famous or ordinary, real or imaginary persons

Rituals

Festivals · Weddings · Funerals · Religious practices

Values — Core of Culture

Social principles · Goals · Standards

Key distinction: Symbols are the most superficial and easiest to perceive, while values form the core of culture and are difficult for outsiders to interpret.

The presentation identifies benevolence as the core value of traditional Chinese culture, alongside righteousness, manner, wisdom, and credit.

## II. Kluckhohn and Strodtbeck's Value Orientation Model

The model classifies cultural differences through five fundamental orientations.

|Orientation|Central Question|
|---|---|
|Human Nature|What is human nature?|
|Person–Nature|How do humans relate to nature?|
|Time|Which period of time is emphasized?|
|Activity|How do people view activity?|
|Relational|How should people relate to one another?|

### 1. Human Nature Orientation

Central question: Are human beings good, evil, or a mixture of both?

Three classifications are introduced.

#### A. Humans Are Evil but Perfectible

Associated with traditional Western Christian beliefs, particularly the Puritans.

- Human beings are born with original sin.
    
- Evil is regarded as part of human nature.
    
- Human beings can be saved by God.
    
- Human nature can be improved through good actions.
    

Example: Adam and Eve eating the forbidden fruit in the Garden of Eden.

The slides emphasize confession as the first step toward becoming good and associate this approach with guilt culture.

#### B. Humans Are a Mixture of Good and Evil

- Every human being has both a good side and a bad side.
    
- Human nature cannot be trusted completely.
    
- Institutions may therefore require supervision and restrictions.
    

Example: Checks and Balances

American political institutions employ checks and balances. The legislative branch and media journalists monitor government officials to prevent wrongdoing.

#### C. Humans Are Good but Corruptible

Associated with Confucian teaching in China and other Asian countries.

- Human beings are believed to be basically good.
    
- Children are regarded as pure and innocent.
    
- People may become corrupted through contact with society.
    
- Parents, teachers, and political leaders are responsible for protecting morality and acting as models of virtue.
    

Example: Mencius' mother moved three times to find a suitable environment for her son's upbringing.

#### Guilt Culture vs. Shame Culture

|Guilt Culture|Shame Culture|
|---|---|
|Associated with the United States|Associated with China and Japan|
|Emphasizes guilt and wrongdoing|Emphasizes shame and social judgment|
|Confession is important|Moral criticism by society is emphasized|

### 2. Person–Nature Orientation

Central question: Should humans submit to, live in harmony with, or control nature?

#### A. Subjugation to Nature

Core belief: Nature controls human beings.

- Natural forces are beyond individual control.
    
- People must accept fate and respect nature.
    
- Humility is important when facing powerful natural forces.
    

Examples: India and parts of South America.

The slides mention earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, and floods as possible influences on this orientation.

#### B. Harmony with Nature

Core belief: Human beings should live as part of nature rather than attempt to dominate it.

- Follow the rhythms of nature.
    
- Maintain balance.
    
- Recognize the relationship between nature and human affairs.
    

Examples: China, Japan, and Native American cultures.

The presentation illustrates this orientation through Chinese expressions such as 天人感应, 天理人情, and 天从人愿.

#### C. Mastery over Nature

Core belief: Human beings can control and transform nature.

- Values technology, change, and science.
    
- Emphasizes solving problems through knowledge.
    
- Views humans and nature as separate.
    
- Attempts to direct natural forces for human benefit.
    

Examples: Western societies, particularly North America.

The slides connect this orientation with exploration, the Industrial Revolution, and technological development.

They also identify concerns about environmental pollution, resource exploitation, technological morality, and species diversity, emphasizing the need for ecological awareness and environmental protection.

### 3. Time Orientation

Central question: Is the past, present, or future most important?

#### A. Past Orientation

- Values history, tradition, religion, and cultural heritage.
    
- Respects established practices.
    
- Uses past experiences as important references.
    

Examples: China, Japan, Britain, France, and Belgium.

#### B. Present Orientation

- Emphasizes the immediate moment.
    
- Regards the future as uncertain.
    
- Favors spontaneity and a relaxed lifestyle.
    
- Places relatively less importance on achievement, as indicated in the presentation's review question.
    

Examples: The Philippines, Spain, and Latin American cultures.

#### C. Future Orientation

- Emphasizes progress, optimism, and planning.
    
- Encourages goal-setting and taking chances.
    
- Believes that people can influence or control the future.
    

Example: American culture.

The Puritans are described as especially concerned with avoiding wasted time and planning for the future.

#### Chinese–Western Comparison

|Western Values|Chinese Values|
|---|---|
|Future-oriented|Past-oriented|
|Progress and future goals|Tradition and past experiences|
|Linear concept of time|Cyclical concept of time|
|Time is money|Time follows repeating patterns|

The presentation also notes that more Chinese people are becoming future-oriented.

### 4. Activity Orientation

Central question: What kinds of activities are valued?

#### A. Doing Orientation

Core emphasis: Activity and action.

- Values measurable external accomplishments.
    
- Emphasizes planning, problem-solving, and decision-making.
    
- Gives importance to deadlines and schedules.
    
- Encourages a sense of urgency.
    

Example: American culture.

The presentation uses Just Do It, Impossible Is Nothing, and fast food to illustrate this orientation.

#### B. Being Orientation

Core emphasis: Spontaneous expression of human personality.

- Accepts people and events as they develop.
    
- Emphasizes existing desires and the present moment.
    
- Gives importance to interpersonal relationships.
    

Example: Latino cultures.

The slides describe Mexicans as placing value on interpersonal relationships and enjoying conversations with family and friends.

In the Chinese–Western comparison, the slides associate Chinese values with who a person is, including social status, roles, and character.

#### C. Being-in-Becoming Orientation

Core emphasis: Personal development and growth.

- Focuses on the development of the whole person.
    
- Values spiritual development over material achievement.
    
- Emphasizes internal rather than externally measurable goals.
    

Examples: Hinduism, Buddhism, and some forms of Eastern meditation.

#### Key Comparison

|Doing|Being|Being-in-Becoming|
|---|---|---|
|Action|Existence and personality|Growth|
|External achievements|Present experience|Internal development|
|What you do|Who you are|What you become|

### 5. Relational Orientation

Central question: How should individuals relate to other people?

#### A. Hierarchy Orientation

- Obey authority.
    
- Know one's social position.
    
- Respect people according to their status.
    
- Follow guidance from leading figures.
    

Example: China.

The slides describe Chinese society as consisting of multiple hierarchies based on age, family and social position, and academic achievement.

#### B. Group Orientation

- Respond to others' thoughts and feelings.
    
- Emphasize mutual dependence.
    
- Maintain loyalty to the group.
    
- Seek guidance from group members.
    

Example: Native American cultures.

#### C. Individualism Orientation

- Express personal ideas and feelings.
    
- Maintain independence and self-reliance.
    
- Make individual decisions and choices.
    

Example: The United States.

|Hierarchy|Group|Individualism|
|---|---|---|
|Authority|Loyalty|Independence|
|Social position|Mutual dependence|Self-reliance|
|Follow leaders|Follow group guidance|Make personal choices|

## III. Chinese–Western Cultural Comparison

The following is the central comparison repeatedly presented in the slides.

|Value Orientation|Chinese Values|Western Values|
|---|---|---|
|Human Nature|Basically good|Mixture of good and evil|
|Person–Nature|Harmony with nature|Mastery over nature|
|Time|Past orientation|Future orientation|
|Activity|Being|Doing|
|Relational|Hierarchy|Individualism|

These classifications summarize dominant cultural preferences rather than describing all individuals.

## IV. Limitations of the Model

The presentation identifies three limitations:

1. Individual differences: Not everyone within a culture shares the same basic values.
    
2. Cultural change: Cultures and their value orientations change over time.
    
3. Generalization: The classification of basic values represents general cultural tendencies rather than universal rules.
    

A preferred set of solutions may be chosen by most people in a culture, but some individuals will choose different alternatives.

## V. Case Study: Elderly People and Individualism

### Background

Liguo visits an American teacher and learns that her 83-year-old grandmother lives alone because her children have moved away.

### Explanation from the Presentation

The case is discussed through the concept of individualist culture.

- Taking elderly parents into one's household may be viewed as an economic burden and a potential threat to privacy and independence.
    
- Americans still care for elderly people.
    
- Caring for older family members can mean respecting their autonomy.
    
- Preserving independence is important because complete dependence on children may represent a serious personal loss.
    

The case illustrates the distinction between the presentation's descriptions of Chinese hierarchical family relationships and American individualism.
